The short answer: most people who fast for Navratri eat one or two simple meals a day built around fruit, dairy, certain flours, and vegetables considered fast-appropriate — and avoid grains like wheat and rice, lentils, onion, garlic, meat, eggs, and alcohol. The longer answer is that "fast-appropriate" is defined by family and regional custom, not by a single rulebook, and your grandmother's list is a legitimate authority.

## Commonly eaten

- **Fruits and nuts** — all fruit is generally accepted; nuts and raisins are a staple.
- **Dairy** — milk, yoghurt, paneer, ghee. Many people lean heavily on dairy during the fast.
- **Fast flours** — kuttu (buckwheat), singhara (water chestnut), and rajgira (amaranth) flours replace wheat for rotis and puris. None of these are true grains, which is why custom permits them.
- **Sabudana** — tapioca pearls, as khichdi, vada, or kheer. Probably the most recognisable Navratri food in North Indian homes.
- **Certain vegetables** — potato, sweet potato, arbi (colocasia), raw banana, and pumpkin are widely used. Which others count varies by family.
- **Sendha namak** — rock salt replaces ordinary table salt in most fasting kitchens.

## Commonly avoided

Wheat, rice, and other grains; lentils and beans; onion and garlic; ordinary salt; meat, fish, and eggs; alcohol. Many families also avoid turmeric and coriander powder during the fast, and some avoid tomatoes — again, family custom decides.

## Why do the rules differ so much?

Because Navratri fasting is a matter of practice and custom rather than a single scriptural prescription. What counts as fast-appropriate settled differently across regions and communities. In much of North India the pattern above is familiar; in Gujarat, many families eat one full meal after sunset and spend the evening at garba; in Bengal, the same nine nights culminate in Durga Puja, where the emphasis falls on worship and celebration rather than restriction — feasting, not fasting, marks the later days. In South India, Navratri looks different again, with golu displays and Saraswati puja. None of these is the correct version; they are different regional expressions of the same festival.

## How strictly should I fast?

However you sustainably can — and it is worth saying plainly that fasting is optional. Common patterns, from lighter to stricter: skipping only grains at the main meal; one meal a day (with fruit and milk otherwise); fruit and milk only; and, for some, a stricter fast on the first and last days with normal fasting food in between. People who are pregnant, unwell, diabetic, or on medication traditionally adapt or skip the fast, and every tradition makes room for that. A fast that harms your health serves nobody.

## The part nobody tells you

If this is your first year running the observance yourself, the honest secret is that everyone's list came from somewhere — usually a mother or mother-in-law — and asking "what does our family avoid?" is not an admission of failure. It is exactly how the custom has always been passed on.
