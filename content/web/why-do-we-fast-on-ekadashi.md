Ekadashi is the eleventh day of each lunar fortnight, which means it comes around twice a month. For many Hindus — especially in Vaishnava traditions, where the day is dear to Vishnu — it is a day of fasting, lighter eating, and turning attention toward devotion. If your mother or grandmother "keeps Ekadashi," this is what she is keeping.

## The short answer

The fast is understood as an act of devotion and self-discipline: setting food aside for a day to make space for remembrance of the divine. In Vaishnava practice particularly, Ekadashi is observed for Vishnu, and the fast is traditionally paired with prayer, japa, or reading — the point is not hunger, it is attention. Many people also speak of the fast in practical terms: a fortnightly reset for body and mind. Tradition and common sense overlap here, and both framings are genuinely part of how the day is lived.

## What do people actually avoid?

The most widespread rule is **no grains and no beans** on Ekadashi — rice, wheat, dal, and everything made from them. Fruit, milk, yoghurt, nuts, and certain non-grain flours are commonly eaten instead. Observance ranges from a complete fast (some keep even a waterless _nirjala_ fast on particular Ekadashis) to simply skipping rice, which in some regions is the heart of the custom. As with most Hindu fasting, **your family's practice is a legitimate authority** — strictness varies by tradition, region, health, and stage of life, and adapting the fast when pregnant, ill, or elderly is itself traditional.

## Why the eleventh day?

The lunar calendar gives the fortnight its rhythm, and the eleventh day — _ekadashi_ means simply "eleventh" — became the devotional pause in that rhythm. Vaishnava tradition tells of Ekadashi personified, born to protect devotees, and each of the year's Ekadashis has its own name and story (Nirjala, Devshayani, Vaikuntha Ekadashi among the best known). Different Puranic stories are told for different Ekadashis; they are devotional narratives, and honest teaching keeps them distinct from calendar mechanics — the fortnightly date — which is simply how the lunar month works.

## Do all Hindus observe it?

No. Ekadashi is most prominent in Vaishnava households and in regions where Vaishnava devotion runs deep; many Shaiva, Shakta, and Smarta families observe it lightly or not at all, keeping other days instead — Mondays for Shiva, Fridays for the goddess, Sankashti Chaturthi for Ganesha. A family that doesn't keep Ekadashi hasn't lapsed; it simply carries a different thread of the tradition.

## Keeping it for the first time

Start with the widely kept version: skip grains and beans for the day, eat fruit and dairy when you need to, and give ten minutes to something devotional — a prayer you know, a few verses, or simply lighting a diya with attention. A fast kept gently and repeated is worth more than a severe one abandoned by afternoon.
