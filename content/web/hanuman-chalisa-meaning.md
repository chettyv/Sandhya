The Hanuman Chalisa is a forty-verse hymn (chālīsā, from _chālīs_, forty) in praise of Hanuman, composed by the poet-saint **Tulsidas** in the sixteenth century — in Awadhi, a literary language of the Hindi family, not Sanskrit. It is plausibly the most-recited Hindu text in the world: sung at temples, played in kitchens, recited on Tuesdays and Saturdays, whispered before exams and flights and hospital visits.

## What the forty verses actually say

The Chalisa is not a philosophical argument; it is a portrait, painted in sequence:

- **Opening couplets (doha):** Tulsidas bows to his guru and asks for strength, framing everything that follows as praise offered by someone who knows his own limits.
- **Verses on Hanuman's form and virtues:** his radiant body, his courage, his wisdom and learning — he is not praised as muscle alone but as _gyan gun sagar_, an ocean of knowledge and virtue.
- **Verses recounting his deeds:** carrying Rama's ring across the ocean to Sita, burning Lanka, bringing the mountain of healing herbs to save Lakshmana — each deed a compressed episode of the Ramayana, so reciting the Chalisa is also rehearsing that story.
- **Verses on his devotion:** the heart of the hymn. Hanuman's greatness is presented as flowing entirely from his service to Rama — power in service of love, never for its own sake.
- **Verses of petition:** the reciter asks for what Hanuman embodies — freedom from fear, removal of suffering and obstacles, steadiness of mind, and closeness to Rama.
- **Closing doha:** a final request that Hanuman dwell in the reciter's heart.

The traditional promise attached to regular recitation — protection from fear and harm — comes from the hymn's own verses. Treat that as the devotional voice of the text, which is how the tradition itself frames it: the Chalisa is a practice of trust, not a contract.

## Why people recite it

Ask ten reciters and you will hear ten honest answers: courage before something difficult; protection for a traveling family member; a Tuesday discipline inherited from a parent; grief; gratitude; or simply that the rhythm steadies the mind. Hanuman holds a particular place in Hindu devotional life as the being to call on when strength and fearlessness are needed — and because he is himself the perfect devotee, devotion to him is understood as a road that leads onward to Rama.

## Learning it yourself

- **Learn it by ear first.** The Chalisa is an oral text with a strong meter; millions learned it from a parent's voice or a recording long before reading it. Any widely used recording will carry the traditional tune.
- **Small pieces.** Two verses at a time, added to daily, gets the whole hymn memorised in a few weeks — this is how children have always learned it.
- **Tuesday and Saturday** are the days most associated with Hanuman across North India, but there is no rule against any other day. Regional and family practice differs, as always.
- **Understanding deepens it but is not a prerequisite.** Generations have recited it before understanding the Awadhi; meaning tends to arrive through repetition, which is part of the design of devotional verse.
