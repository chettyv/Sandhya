## Shloka

**Devanagari:** नात्यश्नतस्तु योगोऽस्ति न चैकान्तमनश्नतः । / न चाति स्वप्नशीलस्य जाग्रतो नैव चार्जुन ॥6.16॥
**IAST:** nātyaśnatastu yogo'sti na caikāntamanaśnataḥ । / na cāti svapnaśīlasya jāgrato naiva cārjuna ॥6.16॥
**Say it:** NAAT-yush-na-tus-too YO-go-sti na chai-KAAN-ta-ma-NUSH-na-taha / na CHAA-ti svup-na-SHEE-lus-ya JAA-gra-to NY-va CHAAR-joo-na
**Meaning:** Yoga is not for one who eats too much, nor for one who does not eat at all; not for one given to too much sleep, nor for one who stays always awake, Arjuna.

## Word by word

- **na** — not
- **ati-aśnataḥ tu** — for one who eats too much
- **yogaḥ** — yoga
- **asti** — there is
- **na ca** — nor
- **ekāntam anaśnataḥ** — for one who does not eat at all
- **na ca** — nor
- **ati svapna-śīlasya** — for one in the habit of too much sleep
- **jāgrataḥ** — for one who stays always awake
- **na eva ca** — nor indeed
- **arjuna** — O Arjuna

## Meaning

Before any technique, Krishna sets a precondition: moderation. Meditation does not work for a person swinging between extremes — overeating or starving, oversleeping or refusing rest. The verse is strikingly practical: yoga is not built on heroic deprivation any more than on indulgence, and both extremes damage the instrument you practise with, the body-mind itself. Commentators across Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, and Dvaita traditions agree on the call to balance and differ mainly over how the right measure is fixed — by scriptural guideline or by what keeps the body fit for practice — without disputing the point itself. This verse states the problem in the negative; the next verse, 6.17, gives the positive prescription.

## Reflection

Which extreme — too much or too little — is quietly unbalancing your practice right now?
