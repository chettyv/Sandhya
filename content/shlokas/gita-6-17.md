## Shloka

**Devanagari:** युक्ताहारविहारस्य युक्तचेष्टस्य कर्मसु । / युक्तस्वप्नावबोधस्य योगो भवति दुःखहा ॥6.17॥
**IAST:** yuktāhāravihārasya yuktaceṣṭasya karmasu । / yuktasvapnāvabodhasya yogo bhavati duḥkhahā ॥6.17॥
**Say it:** yook-TAA-haa-ra-vi-HAA-rus-ya yook-ta-CHESH-tus-ya KAR-ma-soo / yook-ta-svup-NAA-va-BO-dhus-ya YO-go BHA-va-ti dooh-kha-HAA
**Meaning:** For one measured in food and recreation, measured in effort in all actions, measured in sleep and waking, yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow.

## Word by word

- **yukta-āhāra-vihārasya** — for one whose food and recreation are measured
- **yukta-ceṣṭasya** — whose effort is measured
- **karmasu** — in actions
- **yukta-svapna-avabodhasya** — whose sleep and waking are measured
- **yogaḥ** — yoga
- **bhavati** — becomes
- **duḥkha-hā** — the destroyer of sorrow

## Meaning

The positive half of the moderation teaching: when food, recreation, effort in work, sleep, and waking are all yukta — yoked to the right measure — yoga becomes "the destroyer of sorrow." The claim is bold: a regulated ordinary life is not a boring preamble to spiritual practice; it is what makes practice actually relieve suffering. For daily use, pick the one input most out of measure — usually sleep or food — and adjust it slightly rather than heroically. Traditions read the goal differently — Advaita hears a mind steadied for liberating knowledge, while Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita hear a discipline sustaining meditation on the Lord — but all agree the doorway is moderation.

## Reflection

Where could one measured adjustment — in food, rest, or work — reduce your daily friction?
